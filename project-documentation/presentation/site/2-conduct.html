2bActual milestones and effort per member

The plan was 31 person-days over ten work packages. The team recorded 33 by 20 October and forecasts 36 at submission, split equally across three members. Design finished two days late; extraction and the AWS work still landed in their planned windows.

How the team worked

Work was planned as GitHub issues, each with an outcome, acceptance criteria and a way to verify it, on one project board. Instead of fixed-length sprints the team delivered in waves: waves 0–4 locally, then the AWS bundles, then close-out. Pull requests went into integration branches, with CI on every change. Decisions were recorded as architecture decision records (AD-01 to AD-29) and project decisions. There were no formal sprint reviews or retrospectives; their role was taken by the 9 October planning review, independent plan reviews, an audit on 18 October, and the 20 October freeze with its acceptance checklist.

Questions a professor may ask

Is the project finished?

The committed scope is: all seven use cases run on the live environment with tests and cloud evidence. What remains is presentation work, the final report revision, and the open issues in 2a. Those are reported against the original targets, which were not changed after the measurements came in. Three targets were missed and are reported as misses.

Are the person-day figures real timesheets?

No. The team agreed an equal split and allocated the proposal's work packages to match it. The allocation reflects who led which area, and the overrun is attributed to the packages that grew (the delivery pipeline and close-out fixes). Git history cannot serve as a proxy because every commit went through one shared account. A timesheet would have been more precise; the team judged the agreed allocation sufficient for a three-person module project and recorded its basis openly.

Why did effort exceed the plan, and why the AWS package in particular?

The plan had one AWS deployment. In practice every infrastructure check created and deleted a whole stack (a 25-minute deploy and a 25–40 minute teardown, five times), then decided a persistent environment updated in place was cheaper in time, and built a delivery pipeline to update it automatically. That pipeline, and the bugs it exposed (a database that would not pause, a queue that kept stale messages, script errors), account for the extra 3 pd. The rest of the plan held or came in under.

Why delivery waves instead of sprints?

The project lasted about four weeks with three people, and the work had hard dependencies: the domain model before extraction, extraction before cloud deployment, deployment before cloud experiments. Waves grouped issues by dependency rather than by calendar. The cost is less regular feedback: reviews were event-driven (a planning review, an audit, a freeze checklist) rather than every two weeks.

What slipped, and what did you do about it?

Design finished on 9 October instead of 7 October because the lecturer asked for a detailed domain analysis before the split was confirmed, and the team completed the whole design pack before touching code. Extraction then ran on parallel integration branches and landed on 12–13 October, inside its window. The release-time target slipped too, by a margin: about 10.1–10.3 minutes against 10.

What is the most significant unresolved issue?

End-to-end latency. The short-clip Lambda lane cut the wait in the queue from about 33 s to 0.26 s for small clips, but submit-to-done is still 45 s or more, and 35–80 s per job is spent outside both queue wait and encoding. One mechanism that can add up to a minute is the schedule that publishes Media's commands (3c · Outbox), but whether it explains the gap has not been measured. The next step is per-stage timestamps, not more capacity. Details are in 4 · Lanes.

How did three people keep two services consistent without stepping on each other?

Ownership followed specialism, and the code enforces the boundary: each service has its own CI lane that builds and checks only its images, a build check fails if one service's image can import the other's code, and the shared message contract is pinned by fixture files that any contract change must update in the same pull request. That turned most coordination into a reviewed contract change instead of a meeting.
